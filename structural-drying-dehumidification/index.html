What Structural Drying and Dehumidification Is

Structural drying and dehumidification is the controlled removal of moisture from the parts of a building that hold water after a leak: wood framing, subfloor panels, plaster, concrete edges and the empty space inside walls. Surface mopping does not reach any of that. The work uses air movers to push dry air across wet materials and dehumidifiers to pull the moisture out of the air, so the water leaves the building instead of just spreading around it.

How This Differs From First-Response Work

The first visit after a leak is about stopping the source, removing standing water and protecting contents. That work is covered on our water mitigation page. Drying is the longer second phase, and it runs on a measured schedule instead of a rush.

A building can have no puddles and still hold a lot of water inside its materials. This page covers how that hidden water is found, tracked and cleared. If the loss also needs rebuilding afterward, the water damage repair page explains that stage.

Storm Season and Slow Drying

The county public works department counts the wet season from mid October to mid April, which is when many drying calls follow rain. Damp outdoor air in that stretch makes drying harder, because a dehumidifier has to work against moisture coming back in through open doors and vents. Keep doors and windows closed once equipment is running, and ask the crew how they are controlling the room's humidity.

Signs Water Is Still Inside the Structure

Homeowners usually describe it vaguely: the floor feels cool, the baseboard looks a little swollen, or there is a stale smell in a room that was cleaned last week. Those are clues, not proof. A meter reading is the only reliable answer.

Safety Before Equipment Goes In

Drying equipment draws real power, so the electrical state of the room comes first. Before you phone us, get an electrician or the utility on the line if water has touched an outlet, a switch or the panel, and keep out of the wet area. Never stand in water to reset a breaker.

If you smell gas, leave the house and call from outside. Floodwater or sewage in the building changes the job and the protective gear, so tell dispatch what kind of water you are dealing with.

How the Drying Process Works

A crew starts with moisture mapping. A thermal imaging camera shows cooler, wetter areas on walls and floors, and moisture meters confirm the numbers at each spot. Those readings are recorded as the starting point.

Equipment is then placed to fit the room. Air movers are aimed along wet surfaces, and an LGR dehumidifier pulls water from the air in a closed room. In some conditions a desiccant unit is used instead, since it keeps working in cooler air. Wall cavities may be dried by small drilled holes or by lifting a baseboard, so air can flow inside.

Each day the crew returns, takes the same readings and moves equipment. A room that stays wet at one corner gets a different setup the next day.

Drying Goals and When It Is Done

Drying is done when the readings say so, not when the calendar does. The goal is to bring wet materials back to the level of an unaffected dry reference spot in the same home. Crews record that dry standard on day one and compare every later reading to it.

The EPA covers mold after disasters in a guide that urges drying the house and its contents fast, ideally within 24 to 48 hours. Ask for the reading log at pickup, so you can see the final numbers before the equipment leaves.

Decision Guide: Dry in Place or Open Up

Some materials dry well where they sit and others do not. Three things drive that choice: the water category, how long the material stayed wet and what it is made of.

Per the city's permitting FAQ, a permit is required even for interior demolition, though non-structural cosmetic changes generally need none. If drying means taking out part of a wall, ask the crew and the city Building Division who pulls any permit before the cutting starts.

What Changes the Cost

Price tracks the water category and the square footage affected, along with what the materials are. Equipment volume and the count of drying days also matter, since a wet subfloor needs more setup than a wet carpet edge. Any rebuild afterward is priced as its own scope.

Insurance Notes

Whether a drying bill falls under your policy depends on the cause, since the California Department of Insurance describes flood as a usual exclusion and sudden plumbing failure as a different question. A sudden, accidental leak is often covered, while a slow one often is not. Keep photos, the daily moisture log and receipts, and ask your insurer what it needs before you remove anything.

Mistakes to Avoid While Things Dry

Turning equipment off at night because of noise is common, and it resets progress. Another is closing a wall too early because it feels dry to the hand. ANSI/IICRC S500 is the published industry standard for water damage restoration, so a reasonable question for any contractor is whether the crew works to it.

Do not hang pictures back or repaint until the final readings are in. Moisture sealed under new paint tends to show up again.
