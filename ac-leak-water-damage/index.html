AC Leak Water Damage Repair: Where It Starts

AC leak water damage is moisture released by an air conditioning system that ends up in drywall, insulation, flooring or framing. Cooling coils pull water out of the air, and that condensate is meant to leave through a drain line. When the line clogs or the pan overflows, the water spills into the house, and our licensed and insured crews dry and repair the result.

If water is dripping now, switch the system off at the thermostat, set a bucket under the drip and keep people away from a bulging ceiling. The 24/7 dispatch line is open if the leak is spreading.

How a Condensate Line and Overflow Pan Fail

The indoor unit cools air across a cold coil, and the water that forms drips into a pan and flows out through a drain line. Over time, algae, dust and slime can build up inside that line until it blocks. The pan fills, and the water spills over its edge or finds a seam.

Some systems have a secondary pan or a float switch that shuts the unit off when water rises. If yours does not, a slow clog can run for weeks before anyone notices. A stain that returns every summer is often this.

Why Location Makes These Leaks Hard to Spot

The indoor unit often sits where nobody looks: above a hallway ceiling, in an attic, in a closet or in a garage-level utility space. Water from a unit above a ceiling collects in the insulation and then shows up as a faint brown ring. A unit in a closet can wet the wall behind it and the carpet beside it.

Safety Steps for a Leaking Air Conditioner

Air handlers have electrical parts, and the wiring may sit in a wet attic or closet. Turn the system off at the thermostat first. Should water reach the unit's disconnect or electrical panel, keep your hands off both and contact the utility or 911 before contacting us.

Never step onto attic insulation, since it can hide joists and soaked drywall can give way under weight. Leave the AC itself to an HVAC contractor, who can clear the drain line and test the float switch.

Who Fixes the AC and Who Dries the Building

The air conditioner is repaired by an HVAC contractor, and we do not repair or service cooling equipment. The city's permitting FAQ says installing or modifying heating, ventilation and air conditioning systems requires a permit, so ask whoever replaces a pan, line or unit whether they will pull it.

Our job is the building. We find the wet material with moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras, remove soaked insulation and drywall, and run air movers and LGR dehumidifiers. You are best served when both trades are called the same day, so the leak is stopped and the structure dries.

How We Dry a Ceiling or Closet After a Condensate Leak

Condensate water is usually clean at first, but standing water in a drain pan and insulation can carry grime and growth. We check the extent of the wet area, open the ceiling or wall only where readings call for it, and remove insulation that cannot dry.

Containment barriers and HEPA air scrubbers are used if material has grown mold or if demolition will stir dust. If you already see growth, our mold remediation service handles the cleanup. For the rebuild afterward, see water damage repair.

What Changes the Cost of AC Leak Repairs

The main factors are how long the leak ran, how many square feet of drywall and insulation are wet, and whether flooring or cabinetry sits in the path. Days of drying equipment and the amount of rebuild work also count. A leak caught early costs less than one that ran for a season.

A real figure is not possible until someone has seen the loss, and scope and price get explained ahead of any work. Keep any invoice from the HVAC contractor, since it documents the cause.

Insurance Fit and Who Owns the Problem

The California Department of Insurance guide says sudden and accidental water damage is often covered, but gradual leaks and neglect often are not. A slow condensate leak that ran for months may be treated differently from a pan that overflowed suddenly, so describe the timeline honestly and ask your insurer.

Prevention and Mistakes to Avoid

Have an HVAC contractor flush the condensate line and check the pan and float switch before summer. Do not pour bleach into the line on your own, since a contractor can tell you what is safe for your system. Do not ignore a stain that returns every year.

Another mistake is repainting a ceiling without a moisture reading. The paint hides it while the insulation stays wet. Another is leaving the unit running while it leaks.
